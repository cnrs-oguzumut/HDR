\NewPart{Resumé en français}{}
\hspace{3mm}

L'extrême miniaturisation de la technologie moderne exige une meilleure compréhension de la mécanique non conventionnelle, dominée par les fluctuations, des matériaux à des échelles micro et nanométriques. A ces échelles, la nature de la plasticité médiée par les dislocations change radicalement : les échantillons métalliques submicroniques présentent une limite d'élasticité élevée, cependant corrompue par des fluctuations de déformation intermittentes compromettant les processus de mise en forme et mettant en danger la stabilité structurelle. L' un des objectives  de ma recherche   est de mieux comprendre ces phénomènes en développant des modèles mathématiques en allant  au de là des  outils de la mécanique continue classique comme la  théorie de Landau en grande déformation qui peut représenter adéquatement les symétries particulières des réseaux cristallins ou le milieu de Cosserat. L'une de ma contribution majeure concerne la mise en évidence et la caractérisation de l’intermittence des événements résultant du comportement collectif des défauts (dislocations, joints de grains, macles). Il apporte ainsi une explication physique aux observations expérimentales les plus récentes réalisées sur les grands instruments (ESRF Grenoble). L’innovation de la contribution tient à la conjugaison d’approches discrètes et continues pour aborder la description des phénomènes critiques lors de la déformation de la matière. j'ai a pour cela développé une mécanique des milieux continus raffinée, combinée à la méthode des champs de phase.
Le caractère générique de ces avancées scientifiques se manifeste par les applications variées aux matériaux de l’ingénieur : métaux et alliages, matériaux composites (fibres longues, matrice polymère) matériaux amorphes et cellulaires.

%\section{Fracture in meta-materials}
%Brittle materials fail macroscopically through the catastrophic growth of a system-size crack which usually originates from a microscopic flaw. The material away from the crack tip remains effectively unloaded and the whole breaking process is characterized by extreme strain localization. A prototype of brittle material is shown in Fig.\ref{chains}(a). Mathematically, such localization can be traced to the loss of ellipticity of continuum elasticity, see see Fig. \ref{localisation}. Physically, it is a consequence of material softening which, in turn, is a manifestation of the fundamental nonconvexity of the inter-particle interaction. Ultimate strain localization in brittle materials is the source of a host of undesirable issues from low fracture toughness  to dramatic dynamic effects associated with abrupt fragmentation.
%
%The main challenge in delocalizing brittle fracture is to avoid unstable damage propagation driven by stress concentration. The usual approach is to engineer a set of obstacles or dissipation centers promoting the uniformity of damage. The idea is that individual lock-up mechanisms would cause local saturation of damage and drive subsequent failure elsewhere. Usually cracks are channeled by such mechanisms into configurations where some structural conditions impede their unstable propagation and trap them into stable configurations. The geometry of the implied local reinforcements can be tailored to achieve high energy absorption so that the resulting nominally brittle materials can rival their ductile analogs.
%Many particular strategies for creating effective ductility have been proposed in the literature. If we exclude explicit ’plastification’, an incomplete list of nominally ’brittle’ approaches include toughening by micro-cracking, rigidity mitigation, transformational toughening  and the use of multi-level damage.
%
%In this work we proposed a principally different approach to fracture delocalization involving nonlocal interactions which diminish stress concentration by redistributing the load in a softening material. Such interactions can be transmitted by a system of effective backbones whose elasticity is bending dominated. We propose a particular mesoscopic design of such fracture delocalizing system which can be viewed as an architectured meta-material, see Fig. \ref{chains}(b). This design has a bio-mimetic nature and structurally imitates some known biological prototypes.

%More precisely, we show that the de-localization of damage can be facilitated if failure in the initially stretching dominated conventional elastic material is accompanied by the progressive switch to bending dominated nonlocal elasticity. More specifically, we develop a prototype model of a meta-material with nominally brittle constituents at the micro-scale which is engineered to transit from the conventional elastic behavior at small level of damage to bending dominated elastic response at large level of damage. We show that such transition ensures that instead of failure localization, anticipated in stretching dominated regime, the bending elasticity redistributes the stress globally so that every single brittle sub-element of such effective material-structure eventually breaks. %\section{Atomistic modeling of grain-boundary migration and solid-solid phase transformations}
%We developed a modeling approach describing  the evolution of particle positions with an overdamped stochastic dynamics. In contrast to Newtonian dynamics, particle positions are treated as stochastic variables which follow a first-order in time dynamics that do not explicitly incorporate high-frequency vibrations of the crystalline grid (phonons), which typically limit to few nanoseconds the time scale of classical Molecular Dynamics. The chaotic nature of the Newtonian dynamics, which in the long time drives the system to a stochastic equilibrium state, is recovered in the first-order in time dynamics through the use of an additive noise term, carefully chosen to guarantee that the system converges to the correct thermodynamical state in the long-time limit. We applied this method to the to study the shrinking of a circular grain embedded inside a crystal \textit{Overdamped Langevin Dynamics simulations of grain boundary motion}”, Materials Theory, 3,4 (2019) and BCC-to-HCP phase phase transformation observed in Titanium, see Fig. \ref{ptt} .  The collaboration is structured by the co-supervision of a doctoral student (C. Baruffi) funded by ONERA/Université Sorbonne Paris Nord. The experimental studied on both grain boundary  migration and phase transformations has been studied in LSPM, which are now supported by modeling approaches.
